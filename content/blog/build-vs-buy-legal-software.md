The legal technology market is full of vendors, each promising to fix the same handful of problems. For a firm, the real question is whether to **buy, build, or buy-and-extend**.

## Buy when…

- Your needs are standard (time, billing, matters, calendar).
- You want a vendor to handle updates, compliance changes and support.
- You don't have anyone to own custom software afterwards.

## Build when…

- Your process is distinctive: specialist intake, bespoke fee structures, partner-referrer workflows.
- You're paying for a big platform but using 10% of it.
- You need integrations the vendor doesn't offer.
- Data control or hosting requirements rule out standard vendors.
- The software is part of your **competitive advantage** or a product you sell.

## The middle path: buy and extend

Often the best answer is to keep your core system and add **automations and small custom tools** around it: an intake form feeding your matter system, a document generator, a dashboard pulling billing data, a reminder bot for unbilled time. You get the stability of the vendor with the fit of custom work.

## Questions a general counsel or managing partner should ask

1. What problem, in measurable terms, are we solving?
2. What will we do manually if this fails?
3. Who owns the data and can we export it all?
4. What are the confidentiality and AI-training terms?
5. What's the total cost over three years, including people time?

## Frequently asked questions

### Should a law firm build custom software?
Only when the workflow is distinctive, volume is high enough to justify it, or integration and data control matter more than a standard feature set. Otherwise buy and configure.

### What does custom legal software cost?
Scope drives cost. A focused tool (intake flow, document generator, matter dashboard) is a small project; a full practice management system is much larger. Start with a pilot.
