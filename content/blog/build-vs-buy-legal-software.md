<p class="lede">For most law firms, the right answer is <strong>buy the core, extend the edges</strong>: use established legal software for standard functions (time, billing, trust accounting, matters, documents) and add <strong>automations and small custom tools</strong> where your workflow is distinctive. Build fully custom software only when the process is genuinely unique, high-volume, central to your competitive advantage, or when integration and data-control needs rule out standard vendors.</p>

The legal technology market is full of vendors, each promising to fix the same handful of problems, and AI has multiplied the options. Meanwhile, AI-assisted development has made custom software faster and cheaper to build than ever. So the build-vs-buy question deserves a fresh, structured answer. This guide gives you a decision framework, a total-cost comparison, the middle path, the questions to ask, and how to choose a technology partner.

## Buy when…

- **Your needs are standard:** time recording, billing, trust accounting, matters, calendaring, documents.
- **Compliance is complex and changing:** trust accounting rules, e-billing standards, court rules. Vendors absorb those updates for many customers.
- **You want a vendor to handle** updates, security, uptime and support.
- **You don't have anyone to own custom software afterwards.**
- **Speed matters** and a proven product can be live in weeks.
- **You benefit from an ecosystem:** integrations, user communities, trained staff in the market.

## Build when…

- **Your process is distinctive:** specialist intake, high-volume claims, bespoke fee structures, partner-referrer workflows, insurer or government portal integrations.
- **You're paying for a big platform but using 10% of it,** and still working around it.
- **You need integrations the vendor doesn't offer,** or real-time data flows between systems.
- **Data control or hosting requirements** rule out standard vendors (specific data residency, client mandates, sensitive matter types).
- **The software is part of your competitive advantage** (faster, cheaper or better service than competitors) or **a product you sell** (e.g., a legal document service or client-facing platform).
- **Volume is high enough** that efficiency gains justify the investment.

## The middle path: buy and extend

Often the best answer is to keep your core system and add **automations and small custom tools** around it:

- An **intake form** that qualifies enquiries and opens matters in your practice management system
- A **document generator** for your most common documents
- A **dashboard** pulling billing and matter data for partners
- A **reminder bot** for unbilled time and deadlines
- **AI assistants** grounded in your precedents and know-how
- **Integrations** between your practice management system, CRM, accounting, e-signature and client portals
- **Client-facing status updates** triggered by matter events

You get the stability and compliance of the vendor with the fit of custom work. Most modern legal platforms offer APIs and webhooks that make this practical. (See [Legal workflow automation](/blog/legal-workflow-automation/) and [What is a webhook?](/blog/what-is-a-webhook/))

## Total cost of ownership: compare honestly

| Cost | Buy | Build |
|---|---|---|
| **Upfront** | Implementation, migration, configuration, training | Discovery, design, development, testing, migration, training |
| **Ongoing** | Per-user subscription (rises with headcount), add-ons, price increases | Hosting, maintenance, security updates, support, enhancements |
| **Hidden** | Workarounds, double entry, unused modules, vendor lock-in | Key-person risk, technical debt, compliance changes you must implement |
| **Opportunity** | Time lost bending processes to the tool | Time to build before value is realised |

**Rule of thumb:** compare **three-year total cost including people's time**, not just licence fees vs development quotes. Include the cost of workarounds you live with today.

## How AI changes the build-vs-buy decision

- **Vendors are adding AI quickly:** many practice management, document and research tools now include AI features (often as paid add-ons). Check data terms and whether features are mature.
- **Custom AI is more accessible:** firms can build private assistants on enterprise model APIs grounded in their own precedents, with control over data and access. (See [AI for lawyers](/blog/ai-for-lawyers/).)
- **AI-assisted development** has reduced the cost and time of building focused custom tools, making "build the edges" more attractive.
- **Agentic workflows** (AI handling multi-step tasks with human approval) often require integration across systems, which custom work does well. (See [What are agentic workflows?](/blog/what-are-agentic-workflows/))

## Questions a general counsel or managing partner should ask

1. **What problem, in measurable terms, are we solving?** (e.g., "Intake response time from 26 hours to under 1 hour.")
2. **What will we do manually if this fails?**
3. **Who owns the data, and can we export all of it** in usable formats?
4. **What are the confidentiality, security and AI-training terms?**
5. **What's the total cost over three years,** including people's time?
6. **Who will own and maintain it** (internally or with a partner)?
7. **How will it integrate** with our existing systems?
8. **How will we measure success,** and when will we review it?
9. **What's the exit plan** if the vendor or partner relationship ends?

## Evaluating legal tech companies (vendors)

- **Fit:** demo with your real matter types and workflows, not the vendor's script.
- **References:** speak to firms of similar size and practice mix.
- **Security:** certifications (SOC 2, ISO 27001), encryption, MFA, data residency, incident history.
- **Roadmap and stability:** funding, ownership, product investment, support quality.
- **Integration:** APIs, webhooks and native integrations.
- **Contract terms:** price increases, minimum terms, data ownership and exit support.

## Working with a technology partner for custom work

A good developer for a law firm:

- **Starts with your workflow,** not a tech stack
- **Is open about when an off-the-shelf tool is the better choice**
- **Understands confidentiality** and professional obligations
- **Scopes a small, valuable pilot first,** delivered in weeks
- **Builds with security, logging and error handling** from day one
- **Hands over documentation and access,** so you're not locked in
- **Measures outcomes** against the original problem

**Red flags:** large upfront projects without a pilot, vague scopes, no maintenance plan, ownership of code or data retained by the developer, and promises that AI will "replace" processes without human review.

## Example decisions

| Situation | Recommendation |
|---|---|
| 8-lawyer family law firm, standard billing | **Buy** practice management; add automated intake and document templates |
| Personal injury firm handling 3,000 claims/year with referrer partners and insurer portals | **Buy core + build** custom intake, referrer portal and claim workflow automation |
| In-house team drowning in NDA and supplier contract requests | **Buy** a CLM or **build** a lean self-service request and template workflow |
| Firm launching an online legal document product | **Build** (the software is the product), with legal and regulatory advice on the model |
| Firm using 10% of an expensive enterprise platform | **Review:** downgrade, switch to a simpler tool, or build focused tools around the parts used |

## Frequently asked questions

### Should a law firm build custom software?
Only when the workflow is distinctive, volume is high enough to justify it, the software creates competitive advantage, or integration and data-control needs matter more than a standard feature set. Otherwise buy and configure, and add custom automations at the edges.

### What does custom legal software cost?
Scope drives cost. A focused tool such as an intake flow, document generator or matter dashboard is a small project; a full practice management system is much larger. Start with a pilot and compare three-year total cost of ownership against buying.

### What is the buy-and-extend approach?
Keeping an established legal software platform for core functions and adding automations, integrations and small custom tools around it to fit the firm's specific workflows.

### How do I choose a legal tech vendor?
Demo with your real workflows, speak to references from similar firms, check security certifications and data terms, review integration options and contract terms (including data export and price increases), and run a pilot before committing long term.

### Can AI replace legal software?
Not on its own. AI enhances legal software with drafting, summarisation, extraction and automation, but firms still need reliable systems of record for matters, documents, time, billing and trust accounting.

### Who owns custom software built for a law firm?
That depends on the contract. Make sure your agreement gives the firm ownership of (or a perpetual licence to) the code and full ownership of its data, with documentation and handover provisions.
