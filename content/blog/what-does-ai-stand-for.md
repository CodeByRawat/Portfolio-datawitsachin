<p class="lede"><strong>AI stands for artificial intelligence</strong>: the ability of computer systems to perform tasks that normally require human intelligence, such as understanding language, recognising images, solving problems, making decisions and learning from experience. The term was coined in 1955 by computer scientist John McCarthy for a research workshop held at Dartmouth College in 1956.</p>

That's the quick answer. But "AI" is now used to describe everything from your phone's autocorrect to systems that write software and run customer service, so the abbreviation hides a lot. This guide explains what artificial intelligence actually means, where it came from, how it works, the main types, examples you use every day, other things "AI" can stand for, and what AI can and can't do today.

## What does "artificial intelligence" mean?

Break the term into its two parts:

- **Artificial:** made by humans rather than occurring naturally. In this case, created with computers and software.
- **Intelligence:** the ability to learn, understand, reason, solve problems and adapt to new situations.

So artificial intelligence is **human-made systems that show intelligent behaviour**. A more precise definition, from the influential textbook *Artificial Intelligence: A Modern Approach* by Stuart Russell and Peter Norvig, is the study and design of **agents that perceive their environment and take actions to achieve goals**.

In everyday use, people mean something simpler: *software that can do things we used to think only people could do.*

## What exactly is AI? A plain-English explanation

Traditional software follows explicit instructions written by a programmer: *if the customer's balance is below zero, show a warning.* Every rule is spelled out.

AI is different in one crucial way: **most modern AI learns its behaviour from data instead of being told every rule.** You don't write rules for recognising a cat; you show the system millions of labelled images and it learns the patterns that distinguish cats from dogs. You don't write grammar rules for a chatbot; you train a model on vast amounts of text and it learns how language works.

This is why AI can handle messy, real-world tasks (understanding speech, reading handwriting, answering questions in plain English) that were nearly impossible to program with hand-written rules.

## A short history of AI

| Year | Milestone |
|---|---|
| **1950** | Alan Turing publishes "Computing Machinery and Intelligence", asking "Can machines think?" and proposing what became known as the Turing Test |
| **1955–56** | John McCarthy coins "artificial intelligence"; the Dartmouth workshop launches AI as a research field |
| **1960s–70s** | Early programs solve logic problems and play games; early chatbot ELIZA (1966) imitates a therapist |
| **1970s & late 1980s** | "AI winters": funding collapses when early promises aren't met |
| **1980s** | Expert systems encode human expertise as rules for business use |
| **1997** | IBM's Deep Blue beats world chess champion Garry Kasparov |
| **2012** | Deep learning breakthrough: a neural network (AlexNet) dramatically improves image recognition |
| **2016** | DeepMind's AlphaGo beats Go champion Lee Sedol |
| **2017** | Google researchers publish the Transformer architecture ("Attention Is All You Need"), the basis of today's language models |
| **2020–21** | DeepMind's AlphaFold solves a 50-year challenge in predicting protein structures |
| **2022** | ChatGPT launches and brings generative AI to the mainstream |
| **2023–24** | Rapid growth of large language models: Claude, GPT-4, Gemini, Llama; AI becomes multimodal (text, images, audio) |
| **2025–26** | **AI agents** that take actions with tools; AI coding agents; open standards like the Model Context Protocol; widespread business adoption |

## How does AI work?

Most AI today is built with **machine learning**. Here is the basic process:

1. **Collect data:** examples of the task, such as images, text, transactions or sensor readings.
2. **Choose a model:** a mathematical structure, often a neural network with millions or billions of adjustable numbers called *parameters*.
3. **Train:** show the model examples, measure how wrong its outputs are, and adjust the parameters slightly to reduce the error. Repeat billions of times.
4. **Evaluate:** test the model on data it hasn't seen.
5. **Deploy:** use the trained model to make predictions or generate content on new inputs. This is called *inference*.

### How language AI like Claude and ChatGPT works
Large language models (LLMs) are trained on enormous amounts of text to do one thing: **predict the next word** (more precisely, the next *token*, a word or word fragment). Done at massive scale, this simple objective forces the model to learn grammar, facts, reasoning patterns, coding conventions and writing styles. Further training with human feedback teaches it to follow instructions and be helpful and safe.

When you ask a question, the model generates its answer one token at a time, each chosen based on everything that came before. That is why AI writing is fluent, and also why it can sometimes produce confident errors ("hallucinations"): it generates what is *likely*, not what is *verified*.

## The main types of AI

AI is classified in two common ways.

### By capability

| Type | Meaning | Exists today? |
|---|---|---|
| **Narrow AI (weak AI)** | AI that performs specific tasks: translation, image recognition, writing, playing chess | ✅ Yes. All AI today is narrow, though modern models are much broader than before |
| **Artificial general intelligence (AGI)** | AI that matches humans across virtually all cognitive tasks | ❌ Not yet; a major goal and debate in the field |
| **Artificial superintelligence (ASI)** | AI that greatly exceeds human abilities in every domain | ❌ Hypothetical |

### By technique

- **Machine learning (ML):** systems that learn from data.
- **Deep learning:** ML using large neural networks with many layers; the basis of most modern AI.
- **Natural language processing (NLP):** understanding and generating human language.
- **Computer vision:** understanding images and video.
- **Generative AI:** creating new content: text, images, audio, video, code. (See [Which task is a generative AI task?](/blog/which-task-is-a-generative-ai-task/))
- **Reinforcement learning:** learning by trial and error with rewards, used in game-playing AI and robotics.
- **Agentic AI:** systems that pursue goals by planning and using tools. (See [What is an AI agent?](/blog/what-is-an-ai-agent/))

## Examples of AI you use every day

- **Smartphones:** face unlock, photo search ("show me beach photos"), autocorrect, voice assistants.
- **Search engines:** understanding what you mean, AI-generated answer summaries.
- **Streaming and shopping:** recommendations on Netflix, YouTube, Spotify, Amazon.
- **Email:** spam filtering, smart replies, priority inbox.
- **Navigation:** traffic prediction and route optimisation in Google Maps or Waze.
- **Banking:** fraud detection that blocks suspicious card transactions.
- **Translation:** Google Translate, DeepL, real-time captions.
- **Work tools:** AI assistants that draft documents, summarise meetings and analyse data.
- **Customer service:** chatbots and AI agents that answer questions and resolve issues. (See [Will AI replace customer service?](/blog/will-ai-replace-customer-service/))

## What AI can and can't do (in 2026)

### What AI does well
- Understand and write natural language in many languages
- Summarise, translate and rewrite text
- Write and debug code
- Recognise images, speech and patterns in data
- Answer questions using large bodies of information
- Generate images, audio and video
- Carry out multi-step tasks using software tools (AI agents)
- Spot anomalies and make predictions from historical data

### Where AI still struggles
- **Reliability:** it can make confident mistakes; important outputs need checking.
- **Up-to-date facts:** models only know what they were trained on unless connected to search or live data.
- **True understanding of the physical world:** robotics and common-sense physical reasoning lag behind language abilities.
- **Accountability and values:** AI doesn't take responsibility; humans must decide what is acceptable.
- **Novel, high-stakes judgement:** especially when context is missing or ethics are involved.

## Is AI dangerous?

AI brings real benefits and real risks. Current concerns include misinformation and deepfakes, bias in automated decisions, privacy, security misuse, job disruption, and over-reliance on systems that can be wrong. Longer-term, researchers debate the risks of increasingly capable autonomous systems. Governments are responding with regulation, such as the EU AI Act, and AI developers invest in safety research. For individuals and businesses, the practical approach is to use AI with clear human oversight, verify important outputs, and protect sensitive data.

## AI in business: why the abbreviation matters

For businesses, "AI" now mostly means three practical capabilities:

1. **Generative AI** to create and transform content: drafting, summarising, translating, coding.
2. **Predictive AI** to forecast and detect: demand, churn, fraud, risk.
3. **AI automation and agents** to complete work: handling emails, processing documents, resolving tickets, researching leads.

## Frequently asked questions

### What does AI stand for?
AI stands for artificial intelligence: computer systems that can perform tasks that normally require human intelligence, such as understanding language, recognising images, solving problems and learning from data.

### What does AI stand for in texting or slang?
In most online and texting contexts, AI still means artificial intelligence, often referring to chatbots like ChatGPT or Claude. In meeting notes, "AI" can mean "action item".

### Who invented the term artificial intelligence?
John McCarthy coined the term in a 1955 proposal for the Dartmouth Summer Research Project on Artificial Intelligence, held in 1956 and considered the founding event of AI as a field.

### What exactly is AI in simple words?
AI is software that learns from examples to do things like understand speech, answer questions, recognise pictures, write text and make predictions, instead of following only hand-written rules.

### Is AI the same as machine learning?
No. Machine learning is the main technique used to build AI today: systems that learn from data. AI is the broader goal of making machines intelligent, which can also include rule-based systems.

### What is the difference between AI and generative AI?
AI is the whole field. Generative AI is a branch of AI that creates new content, such as text, images, audio, video or code, rather than only analysing or classifying existing data.

### What are the 4 types of AI?
A commonly cited classification (from Arend Hintze) lists reactive machines, limited memory, theory of mind and self-aware AI. Only the first two exist today; the last two are theoretical. Another common split is narrow AI, general AI (AGI) and superintelligence.
