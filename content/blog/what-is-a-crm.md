<p class="lede"><strong>CRM stands for customer relationship management.</strong> A CRM is software that stores every interaction your business has with customers and prospects (contacts, companies, emails, calls, meetings, deals, support tickets and purchases) in one place, so your sales, marketing and service teams can manage relationships, follow up on time, automate routine work and see what's driving revenue.</p>

If you've ever lost a lead because someone forgot to follow up, had two salespeople call the same prospect, or couldn't answer "how much revenue is in our pipeline?", you've felt the problem a CRM solves. This guide explains what a CRM is in plain English, what CRM software does, the types of CRM, examples, how AI is changing CRM in 2026, what it costs, and how to choose and set one up properly.

## What does CRM mean?

The term **CRM** is used in two related ways:

1. **A strategy:** customer relationship management as a business practice: managing every interaction with customers to build lasting, profitable relationships.
2. **A software system:** the tool that makes that practice possible: a central database plus features for sales, marketing and service.

When people ask "what is a CRM?", they almost always mean the software.

## What is a CRM in simple terms?

Think of a CRM as a **shared, intelligent address book and to-do list for your whole company**:

- **Address book:** every contact and company, with their details, history and relationship to you.
- **Timeline:** every email, call, meeting, purchase and support request, automatically logged.
- **Pipeline:** where each potential deal stands, from first contact to closed.
- **To-do list:** reminders and tasks so nobody forgets to follow up.
- **Reports:** what's working, what's stuck, and what revenue is coming.

Instead of information living in individual inboxes, spreadsheets and people's heads, it lives in one place everyone can see.

## What does CRM software do? Core features

### Contact and account management
Store people (contacts) and organisations (accounts or companies) with details, notes, relationships and full interaction history.

### Lead management
Capture leads from your website, forms, ads, events and chat; score them; assign them to the right person; and track them through qualification.

### Sales pipeline (deal) management
Track opportunities through stages (e.g., *new → qualified → demo → proposal → negotiation → won/lost*) with values, close dates and next steps. Visual pipeline boards show everything at a glance.

### Activity tracking and email integration
Sync email and calendars so emails, meetings and calls are logged automatically. Many CRMs include calling, email templates, open tracking and scheduling links.

### Tasks and reminders
Assign follow-ups, set reminders and see what's due today.

### Marketing automation
Email campaigns, segmentation, nurture sequences, landing pages and forms, often built into the CRM or connected to it.

### Customer service
Support tickets, shared inboxes, knowledge bases and customer satisfaction surveys, linked to the customer record.

### Reporting and forecasting
Dashboards for pipeline, conversion rates, activity, revenue, sales forecasts and team performance.

### Workflow automation
Automate repetitive work: assign leads, create tasks, send emails, update fields, notify teams when something happens.

### Integrations
Connect with email, calendars, accounting, e-commerce, support, marketing, telephony and data tools. (See [What is CRM integration?](/blog/crm-integration-and-automation/))

## How does a CRM work? A simple example

1. A visitor fills in a demo request form on your website.
2. The CRM creates a contact and company record automatically, enriches it with company size and industry, and scores the lead.
3. The lead is assigned to the right sales rep, who gets a notification and a task to call within an hour.
4. The rep's call and follow-up emails are logged automatically on the timeline.
5. The rep creates a deal in the pipeline worth $12,000, stage "Demo booked".
6. Over the following weeks, the deal moves through stages; the manager sees it in the forecast.
7. The deal is won; the CRM triggers an onboarding workflow for the customer success team.
8. Months later, a support ticket from the customer appears on the same record, so the account manager sees it before the renewal call.

Everyone works from the same, complete picture of the customer.

## Types of CRM

| Type | Focus | Example uses |
|---|---|---|
| **Operational CRM** | Automating and managing day-to-day sales, marketing and service processes | Pipelines, lead routing, email campaigns, ticketing |
| **Analytical CRM** | Analysing customer data to understand behaviour and improve decisions | Segmentation, churn prediction, lifetime value, sales forecasting |
| **Collaborative CRM** | Sharing customer information across teams and channels | Shared timelines, handoffs between sales and service, partner portals |

Most modern CRMs combine all three. You'll also see CRMs described by focus: **sales CRM** (pipeline-first), **marketing CRM** (campaigns and automation), **service CRM** (support), and **industry CRMs** (real estate, legal, healthcare, nonprofits, financial advisers).

## Who uses a CRM?

- **Sales teams:** manage leads, pipelines and forecasts.
- **Marketing teams:** segment audiences, run campaigns, measure which channels produce revenue.
- **Customer service teams:** see full customer context when solving issues.
- **Account managers and customer success:** manage renewals and expansion.
- **Leadership:** see pipeline, revenue and customer health in real time.
- **Small businesses and freelancers:** keep track of clients, quotes and follow-ups without a spreadsheet.

You don't need a big team to benefit. If you have more customers or prospects than you can keep track of in your head, you need a CRM.

## Benefits of a CRM

- **No more lost leads or forgotten follow-ups.**
- **One source of truth** for customer information.
- **Better customer experience:** everyone sees the history; customers don't repeat themselves.
- **Higher sales productivity:** less admin, more selling, especially with automation.
- **Accurate forecasting and reporting.**
- **Better marketing ROI:** know which campaigns actually produce customers.
- **Continuity:** when someone leaves, their relationships and history stay with the company.
- **Scalability:** processes that work for 5 people keep working at 50.

## How AI is changing CRM

AI has become one of the main reasons companies upgrade or switch CRMs. Practical AI capabilities in 2026:

- **Automatic data capture:** emails, calls and meetings logged and summarised without manual entry.
- **Call and meeting summaries** with next steps and key fields extracted into the CRM.
- **Lead and deal scoring** that predicts likelihood to convert or close.
- **Email drafting** grounded in the customer's history.
- **Natural-language reporting:** "Show me deals over $20k closing this quarter with no meeting booked."
- **Research agents** that brief reps before meetings.
- **AI agents** that qualify inbound leads, answer customer questions, update records and handle routine service tasks.

The catch: **AI is only as good as your CRM data.** Duplicate records, missing fields and unlogged activity make AI outputs unreliable. Clean data is the foundation for every AI feature. (For more, see [How to automate your sales process](/blog/how-to-automate-your-sales-process/).)

## How much does a CRM cost?

- **Free plans:** HubSpot, Zoho and others offer free tiers suitable for very small teams.
- **Small business plans:** typically about $15–$50 per user per month.
- **Professional/mid-market plans:** roughly $50–$150 per user per month, with automation, reporting and more customisation.
- **Enterprise plans:** $150–$300+ per user per month, plus add-ons for AI, marketing contacts, service and analytics.
- **Implementation:** from DIY setup for small teams to tens of thousands of dollars for complex enterprise implementations, data migration and integrations.

Watch for costs that scale with marketing contacts, AI credits, and premium integrations. Check official pricing pages, which change often.

## How to choose a CRM

1. **Define your process first:** how leads arrive, stages in your sales process, handoffs to service, what you need to report on.
2. **List must-have features** and integrations (email, calendar, accounting, e-commerce, support, phone).
3. **Consider your team:** ease of use matters more than feature count, because a CRM nobody updates is worthless.
4. **Check data and AI capabilities:** automatic activity capture, AI features, and whether AI pricing is included.
5. **Check scalability and total cost** over 3 years, including add-ons.
6. **Trial two or three options** with real data and real users.
7. **Check security and compliance:** permissions, SSO, data residency, audit logs, GDPR tools.

## How to set up a CRM so people actually use it

Most CRM failures are adoption failures, not software failures.

1. **Start simple:** core objects (contacts, companies, deals), a clear pipeline, and a few required fields.
2. **Import and clean your data:** deduplicate, standardise, and archive what's outdated.
3. **Connect email and calendar** so activity is captured automatically.
4. **Define the rules:** what counts as a lead, what each deal stage means, who owns what.
5. **Automate the boring parts:** lead assignment, follow-up tasks, reminders, data entry from calls.
6. **Build the reports leaders will use** in weekly meetings, so updating the CRM becomes the way work gets reviewed.
7. **Train the team** with their real workflows, and gather feedback after the first month.
8. **Review and improve quarterly.**

## Frequently asked questions

### What is a CRM?
A CRM (customer relationship management) system is software that stores and organises all your interactions with customers and prospects (contacts, emails, calls, deals and support tickets) so teams can manage relationships, automate follow-ups and track sales and revenue.

### What does CRM stand for?
CRM stands for customer relationship management.

### What is a CRM used for?
A CRM is used to manage contacts and companies, capture and route leads, track sales pipelines, log communications, automate follow-ups and marketing, support customers, and report on sales and revenue.

### What is an example of a CRM?
Popular examples include Salesforce, HubSpot, Microsoft Dynamics 365, Zoho CRM, Pipedrive and Freshsales.

### Do small businesses need a CRM?
Yes, once you have more customers and prospects than you can reliably track in your head or a spreadsheet. Free and low-cost CRMs make it affordable for small businesses and even freelancers.

### What is the difference between CRM and ERP?
A CRM manages customer-facing activities (sales, marketing and service). An ERP (enterprise resource planning) system manages back-office operations such as finance, inventory, procurement and manufacturing. They are often integrated so customer orders flow into operations and finance.

### How do I use a CRM?
Add your contacts and companies, connect your email and calendar, create deals for each sales opportunity and move them through pipeline stages, log or automatically capture activities, set tasks for follow-ups, and use dashboards to review pipeline and performance regularly.
