<p class="lede"><strong>Project planning</strong> is the process of defining <strong>what</strong> a project will deliver, <strong>how</strong> and <strong>when</strong> the work will be done, <strong>who</strong> will do it, <strong>what it will cost</strong>, and <strong>what could go wrong</strong>, before and during execution. The output is a <strong>project plan</strong>: an agreed roadmap covering goals, scope, deliverables, tasks, schedule, resources, budget, risks and communication, used to guide and measure progress.</p>

Good planning is the biggest single predictor of whether a project finishes on time and on budget. It doesn't mean predicting every detail; it means making the important decisions, assumptions and trade-offs explicit, so the team and stakeholders are aligned and surprises are caught early. This guide walks through the steps to create a project plan, compares Waterfall and Agile planning, shares a template, recommends tools, and shows how AI can speed up planning.

## Why project planning matters

- **Alignment:** everyone agrees what's being delivered and why
- **Realism:** exposes whether the deadline and budget are achievable
- **Coordination:** shows dependencies and who needs to do what, when
- **Risk management:** identifies problems before they happen
- **Control:** provides a baseline to measure progress and manage changes
- **Communication:** gives stakeholders a clear picture

## The 10 steps of project planning

### 1. Define the goal and success criteria
Why does the project exist? What outcome will make it a success, in measurable terms? *"Launch the new customer portal by 31 March, reducing support calls about order status by 30%."*

### 2. Identify stakeholders
Who is affected, who decides, who contributes, who needs to be informed? Note their interests and influence. A simple **RACI** (Responsible, Accountable, Consulted, Informed) clarifies roles.

### 3. Define scope (and what's out of scope)
List deliverables and requirements. Just as important: write down what is **not** included, to prevent scope creep. Record key **assumptions** and **constraints** (budget, deadline, technology, regulations).

### 4. Break down the work (WBS)
Create a **work breakdown structure**: divide deliverables into smaller work packages and tasks until each is small enough to estimate and assign (often a few days of work).

### 5. Estimate effort and duration
Estimate how long each task will take, ideally with the people doing the work. Techniques include expert judgement, comparison with past projects, three-point estimates (optimistic, most likely, pessimistic) and, in Agile, story points.

### 6. Sequence tasks and map dependencies
Which tasks depend on others? Identify the **critical path**: the longest chain of dependent tasks, which determines the earliest finish date. Delays on the critical path delay the project.

### 7. Build the schedule
Put tasks on a timeline (a **Gantt chart** or roadmap), with milestones, and add contingency buffers for uncertainty. Check against team availability, holidays and other commitments.

### 8. Plan resources and budget
Assign people and equipment; check capacity. Build the budget from labour, materials, software, vendors and contingency.

### 9. Identify and plan for risks
List risks, assess likelihood and impact, decide responses (avoid, reduce, transfer, accept) and assign owners. Track them, along with assumptions, issues and dependencies, in a **RAID log**. (See [What is a RAID log?](/blog/what-is-a-raid-log/))

### 10. Plan communication and governance
Who gets which updates, how often, in what format? How will decisions and changes be approved? Define status reports, meetings and escalation paths.

**Then:** get the plan approved, set the **baseline**, and use it to track progress, updating it as things change through a controlled change process.

## What goes into a project plan (template)

| Section | Contents |
|---|---|
| **Overview** | Purpose, goals, success criteria, sponsor |
| **Scope** | Deliverables, requirements, out of scope, assumptions, constraints |
| **Stakeholders & roles** | Stakeholder list, RACI |
| **Work breakdown** | Phases, deliverables, tasks |
| **Schedule** | Timeline, milestones, dependencies, critical path |
| **Resources** | Team, roles, capacity, vendors |
| **Budget** | Cost estimates, contingency, approval levels |
| **Risks** | Risk register / RAID log |
| **Quality** | Acceptance criteria, reviews, testing |
| **Communication** | Reporting cadence, meetings, channels |
| **Change control** | How scope, time or budget changes are requested and approved |

Smaller projects can fit this on one or two pages; larger ones need more detail. Match the plan's weight to the project's size and risk.

## Waterfall vs Agile planning

| | Waterfall (predictive) | Agile (adaptive) |
|---|---|---|
| **Approach** | Plan the whole project up front, then execute in sequence | Plan at a high level, then plan in detail in short iterations (sprints) |
| **Scope** | Fixed early | Evolves based on feedback |
| **Best for** | Clear requirements, regulated or physical projects (construction, compliance, hardware) | Uncertain requirements, software, innovation |
| **Planning artefacts** | Detailed WBS, Gantt chart, baseline | Product roadmap, backlog, sprint plans, release plans |
| **Change** | Controlled through change requests | Expected and welcomed between iterations |

Many organisations use **hybrid** planning: a high-level phased plan with milestones and budget, and Agile delivery within phases.

## How AI helps with project planning

AI assistants and AI features in project tools can now:

- **Draft a first project plan** from a short description: goals, phases, WBS, milestones and risks
- **Suggest tasks and checklists** based on similar projects
- **Estimate durations** from historical data in your project tool
- **Identify risks and dependencies** you may have missed
- **Generate RACI charts and communication plans**
- **Summarise meetings** into decisions and action items, updating the plan
- **Draft status reports** from task progress
- **Flag schedule risks** as work slips

**Example prompt:**
> "I'm planning a 12-week project to launch a customer self-service portal for a 50-person logistics company. Team: 1 PM, 2 developers, 1 designer, 1 ops lead. Create a project plan with goals, success metrics, phases, a work breakdown structure, milestones, key dependencies, top 10 risks with mitigations, and a RACI. Assume a hybrid approach with 2-week sprints."

Treat the result as a **first draft**: review estimates with the team, adapt to your reality, and own the decisions. (More: [How to track tasks at work](/blog/how-to-track-tasks-at-work/) and [How to automate tasks with AI](/blog/how-to-automate-tasks-with-ai/).)

## Common project planning mistakes

1. **Vague goals** with no measurable success criteria
2. **No "out of scope" list,** leading to scope creep
3. **Planning without the people doing the work**
4. **Optimistic estimates with no contingency**
5. **Ignoring dependencies** on other teams and vendors
6. **No risk planning**
7. **Over-planning:** detailed plans for uncertain work that will change anyway
8. **Treating the plan as fixed:** not updating it as reality changes
9. **No change control:** accepting every new request without assessing impact

## Why is project management important?

Project planning is one part of project management. Together, they increase the likelihood that projects deliver their intended value by aligning stakeholders, using resources efficiently, managing risks, controlling costs and timelines, and ensuring quality. Without them, organisations waste money on late, over-budget or failed projects.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is project planning?
Project planning is defining a project's goals, scope, deliverables, tasks, schedule, resources, budget, risks and communication approach, producing a project plan that guides execution and provides a baseline to track progress.

### What are the steps in project planning?
Define goals and success criteria, identify stakeholders, define scope, create a work breakdown structure, estimate tasks, sequence tasks and dependencies, build the schedule, plan resources and budget, plan for risks, and plan communication and governance.

### What should a project plan include?
An overview with goals and success criteria, scope and exclusions, stakeholders and roles, work breakdown, schedule and milestones, resources, budget, risks, quality criteria, communication plan and change control process.

### What is the difference between a project plan and a project schedule?
A project schedule is the timeline of tasks and milestones. A project plan is broader, covering scope, goals, resources, budget, risks, communication and governance, with the schedule as one component.

### Can AI create a project plan?
AI can draft project plans, work breakdowns, risk lists, RACI charts and timelines from a description, and can update plans and status reports from project data. Project managers should review and adapt the output with the team.

### What is the critical path in project planning?
The critical path is the longest sequence of dependent tasks in a project, which determines the shortest possible project duration. Any delay to a task on the critical path delays the whole project unless recovered.
