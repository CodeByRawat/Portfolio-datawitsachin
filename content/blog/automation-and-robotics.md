<p class="lede"><strong>Automation</strong> is using technology to perform tasks with minimal human involvement, whether in software (workflows, RPA, AI) or in the physical world (machines and control systems). <strong>Robotics</strong> is the field of designing, building and programming <strong>robots</strong>: physical machines that sense their environment, make decisions and move to carry out tasks. Robotics is one way to achieve automation in the physical world; not all automation uses robots, and increasingly robots use AI to handle tasks that used to require people.</p>

"Automation and robotics" (or "robotics and automation") is a common name for engineering degrees, industry sectors and job roles, and it's at the centre of a new wave of investment as AI makes robots more flexible. This guide explains what each term means, how they differ, the main types of robots and where they're used, how AI is changing robotics, how software "robots" (RPA) fit in, careers, and how businesses can get started.

## Automation vs robotics: what's the difference?

| | Automation | Robotics |
|---|---|---|
| **Definition** | Technology performing tasks with minimal human intervention | Machines that sense, decide and physically act |
| **Scope** | Software and physical processes | Mostly physical tasks |
| **Examples** | Workflow software, RPA bots, conveyor systems, automated valves, thermostats, AI document processing | Robotic arms, warehouse robots, surgical robots, drones, robot vacuums, humanoids |
| **Requires a robot?** | No | Yes |
| **Relationship** | Broad goal | One important way to achieve physical automation |

**Example:** a bottling plant uses **automation** (sensors, programmable logic controllers, conveyors, filling machines) and **robots** (arms that pack bottles into cases and stack pallets). An accounting team uses **automation** with no physical robots at all: software that processes invoices. (See [What is software automation?](/blog/what-is-software-automation/))

## Types of automation

- **Fixed (hard) automation:** dedicated equipment for high-volume, single-product production (e.g., automotive stamping lines)
- **Programmable automation:** equipment reprogrammed for different batches
- **Flexible automation:** quickly adapts to different products with minimal downtime
- **Process automation / industrial control:** continuous processes in chemicals, energy, water (PLC, SCADA, DCS systems)
- **Software and business process automation:** workflows, RPA, AI (see [How to automate business processes](/blog/how-to-automate-business-processes/))

## Types of robots

| Type | Description | Common uses |
|---|---|---|
| **Industrial robot arms** (articulated, SCARA, delta) | Programmable arms with several joints | Welding, painting, assembly, pick-and-place, packaging |
| **Collaborative robots (cobots)** | Smaller robots designed to work safely alongside people | Assembly, machine tending, quality inspection in small and mid-sized manufacturers |
| **Autonomous mobile robots (AMRs)** | Navigate independently using sensors and maps | Moving goods in warehouses, hospitals, factories |
| **Automated guided vehicles (AGVs)** | Follow fixed paths | Material transport in plants |
| **Warehouse robotic systems** | Goods-to-person systems, robotic picking, sorting | E-commerce fulfilment (see [What is supply chain automation?](/blog/what-is-supply-chain-automation/)) |
| **Service robots** | Perform tasks for people outside factories | Cleaning, delivery, hospitality, security |
| **Medical and surgical robots** | High-precision assistance to surgeons; rehabilitation; pharmacy automation | Minimally invasive surgery, lab automation |
| **Agricultural robots** | Harvesting, weeding, milking, monitoring | Farming and greenhouses |
| **Drones (UAVs)** | Flying robots | Inspection, mapping, delivery, agriculture, inventory counts |
| **Humanoid robots** | Human-shaped general-purpose robots | Emerging pilots in logistics and manufacturing |
| **Consumer robots** | Robot vacuums, lawn mowers | Homes |

## Where automation and robotics are used

### Manufacturing
The largest user of industrial robots: automotive, electronics, metals, plastics, food and beverage. The International Federation of Robotics (IFR) reports millions of industrial robots operating worldwide, with Asia (especially China) installing the most each year and countries such as South Korea, Singapore and Germany having the highest robot density relative to manufacturing workers.

### Logistics and warehousing
AMRs, robotic picking arms, automated storage and retrieval systems (AS/RS), sortation and palletising robots, driven by e-commerce growth and labour shortages.

### Healthcare
Surgical robots, pharmacy and lab automation, hospital delivery robots, rehabilitation and exoskeletons, and disinfection robots. (See [Automation in healthcare](/blog/automation-in-healthcare/).)

### Agriculture
Robotic milking, autonomous tractors, precision spraying and weeding, harvesting robots for fruit and vegetables, and drones for crop monitoring.

### Construction and inspection
Drones for surveying and inspection, bricklaying and layout robots, robotic inspection of pipes, power lines and infrastructure.

### Retail and hospitality
Inventory-scanning robots, cleaning robots, food preparation automation, delivery robots.

### Energy and utilities
Inspection robots for wind turbines, pipelines and power plants; automated grid control.

## How AI is changing robotics

Traditional industrial robots follow precisely programmed paths in structured environments; change the product and someone reprograms the robot. AI is making robots more adaptable:

- **Computer vision:** robots see and identify objects, defects and positions, picking items they've never seen before.
- **Learning from demonstration and reinforcement learning:** robots learn skills from human demonstrations or trial and error, often in simulation first. (See [What is reinforcement learning?](/blog/what-is-reinforcement-learning/))
- **Foundation models for robotics:** large models that combine vision, language and action (vision-language-action models) let robots follow natural-language instructions ("put the red parts in the left bin") and generalise across tasks.
- **Simulation and digital twins:** training and testing robots in realistic virtual environments before deployment.
- **Predictive maintenance:** AI predicts robot and equipment failures before they happen.
- **Humanoid robots:** major companies and startups are piloting humanoids in factories and warehouses, aiming for general-purpose robots that work in spaces designed for people. These are still early and far from widespread deployment.

## Software robots: RPA

In business, "robots" often refers to **software bots** in robotic process automation: programs that mimic human actions in computer applications. They're automation, but not robotics in the engineering sense. (See [What is robotic process automation?](/blog/what-is-robotic-process-automation/))

## Benefits of automation and robotics

- **Productivity:** continuous operation, higher throughput
- **Quality and consistency:** precise, repeatable work, fewer defects
- **Safety:** robots take on dangerous, dirty and dull jobs (heavy lifting, toxic environments, repetitive strain)
- **Addressing labour shortages** in manufacturing, logistics and agriculture
- **Flexibility** with modern programmable and AI-enabled systems
- **Data and visibility** from connected equipment

## Challenges

- **Upfront cost** and integration complexity (though robot-as-a-service models and cheaper cobots are lowering barriers)
- **Skills gaps:** programming, maintaining and integrating robots requires trained technicians
- **Unstructured environments** remain hard for robots
- **Workforce impact:** some tasks and jobs are displaced; reskilling is essential
- **Safety and standards compliance** (e.g., ISO 10218 for industrial robots, ISO/TS 15066 for collaborative applications)
- **Cybersecurity** for connected systems

## Careers in automation and robotics

| Role | What they do |
|---|---|
| **Robotics engineer** | Designs and develops robots and robotic systems |
| **Automation / controls engineer** | Designs and programs PLCs, SCADA and control systems |
| **Robot programmer / technician** | Programs, installs and maintains robots |
| **Mechatronics engineer** | Combines mechanical, electrical and software engineering |
| **Computer vision / AI engineer** | Builds perception and learning systems for robots |
| **Systems integrator** | Designs complete automation cells and lines for customers |
| **Maintenance technician** | Keeps automated equipment running |

Related degrees include **robotics and automation engineering**, mechatronics, mechanical, electrical and computer engineering, and computer science.

## How businesses can start with automation and robotics

1. **Identify bottlenecks:** repetitive, dangerous or hard-to-staff tasks; quality problems; throughput limits.
2. **Start with software automation** where processes are information-based: often the fastest ROI. (See [How to build an automation strategy](/blog/how-to-build-an-automation-strategy/).)
3. **For physical tasks, consider cobots or AMRs** for flexible, lower-cost starting points.
4. **Work with a systems integrator** for design, safety assessment and installation.
5. **Calculate ROI:** labour savings, quality, throughput, safety and the costs of equipment, integration, training and maintenance; explore leasing or robot-as-a-service.
6. **Involve and reskill your workforce** from the start.
7. **Pilot, measure, scale.**

## Frequently asked questions

### What is automation and robotics?
Automation is using technology to perform tasks with minimal human involvement, in software or physical processes. Robotics is the design, building and programming of robots, machines that sense, decide and physically act. Robotics is one way to automate physical work.

### What is the difference between automation and robotics?
Automation is the broad goal of performing tasks without human effort and doesn't require robots (e.g., software workflows or automated valves). Robotics specifically involves physical robots that can sense and move to carry out tasks.

### What are examples of robotics and automation?
Robotic arms welding cars, warehouse robots moving goods, surgical robots, robotic milking systems, drones inspecting infrastructure, automated production lines, and software robots processing invoices.

### How is AI used in robotics?
AI gives robots computer vision to recognise objects, learning from demonstrations and trial and error, natural-language instruction following, adaptive control in changing environments and predictive maintenance, making robots more flexible.

### What are cobots?
Cobots (collaborative robots) are robots designed to work safely alongside people, usually smaller and easier to program than traditional industrial robots, making automation accessible to small and mid-sized manufacturers.

### Will robots take over jobs?
Robots and automation take over specific tasks, especially repetitive, dangerous or physically demanding ones, and some jobs change or disappear. They also create jobs in robot programming, maintenance, integration and supervision, and help address labour shortages. Reskilling is key.
