<p class="lede">AI is already useful in legal work and already risky if misused. Lawyers can safely use AI to <strong>draft, summarise, extract, research and organise</strong>, as long as they <strong>protect client confidentiality</strong>, <strong>verify every citation and statement of law</strong>, <strong>supervise the output</strong> like a junior's work, and follow their regulator's guidance. AI will replace many legal <em>tasks</em>, but not the judgement, advocacy and accountability clients pay lawyers for.</p>

AI adoption in law has moved fast. Clio's 2025 Legal Trends Report found **79% of legal professionals now use AI** in some form, with adoption highest in large firms (87%) and still substantial among solo practitioners (71%). Thomson Reuters' 2025 Future of Professionals report found lawyers expect AI to free up roughly **240 hours per year** each. At the same time, courts have dealt with well over a thousand cases worldwide involving AI-fabricated citations, and sanctions are rising.

This guide is a practical playbook: where AI genuinely helps lawyers, the ethics and confidentiality rules, how to avoid the fake-citation trap, how to choose tools, and what the future looks like.

## Where AI genuinely helps lawyers (and law assistants)

### 1. First drafts
Letters, emails, client updates, clauses, memos, demand letters, cover notes, internal summaries. AI produces a competent first draft in seconds; the lawyer supplies judgement, accuracy and tone.

### 2. Summarising
Long contracts, deposition transcripts, witness statements, email chains, medical records, judgments, regulatory updates. Summaries help you triage and prepare, but always verify key points against the source.

### 3. Extracting information
Parties, dates, obligations, notice periods, termination rights, liability caps, governing law, from one contract or hundreds. (See [Document review software](/blog/legal-document-review-software/).)

### 4. Research assistance
Exploring an unfamiliar issue, generating search terms, outlining arguments and counter-arguments, and identifying what to look for. Then go to authoritative sources. Legal research platforms (Westlaw, Lexis+, vLex/Vincent, Bloomberg Law and others) now include AI features grounded in their databases, which reduces (but does not eliminate) the risk of fabricated authorities.

### 5. Comparing and redlining
Comparing a counterparty's draft against your standard positions, highlighting deviations and suggesting fallback language.

### 6. Chronologies and case analysis
Building timelines from documents, identifying inconsistencies across witness statements, and organising evidence.

### 7. Client communication and intake
Plain-English explanations of processes, FAQs, intake questionnaires, appointment handling and status updates. (See [Legal intake software](/blog/legal-intake-software/).)

### 8. Firm knowledge
Private assistants that answer questions from your own precedents, templates, know-how notes and policies, so juniors find the firm's answer instead of starting from scratch.

### 9. Practice operations
Time-entry suggestions, billing narratives, marketing content, reporting and internal admin. (See [Legal billing software](/blog/legal-billing-software/) and [Legal workflow automation](/blog/legal-workflow-automation/).)

## Can lawyers use ChatGPT?

Yes, many do, but **how** matters:

- **Consumer accounts are the wrong place for client data.** Use business or enterprise versions (ChatGPT Business/Enterprise, Claude for Work, Microsoft Copilot with enterprise data protection, Gemini for Workspace) or legal platforms with contractual commitments on confidentiality, data retention and no training on your data.
- **Never treat output as authority.** It's a draft from a fast, fallible assistant.
- **Anonymise where possible,** especially for brainstorming.
- **Know your firm's policy** and your regulator's guidance.

## The ethics rules: what regulators expect

### United States: ABA Formal Opinion 512
In July 2024 the American Bar Association issued **Formal Opinion 512**, its first formal ethics opinion on generative AI. It isn't binding by itself, but it reflects how the Model Rules apply and has been echoed by many state bars. Key points:

| Duty | What it means for AI use |
|---|---|
| **Competence (Rule 1.1)** | Have a reasonable understanding of the capabilities and limitations of the AI tools you use, and review output appropriately |
| **Confidentiality (Rule 1.6)** | Assess the risk that client information will be disclosed or accessed; informed client consent may be needed before inputting confidential information into some tools |
| **Communication (Rule 1.4)** | In some circumstances, tell clients about AI use, particularly where it affects the representation or fees |
| **Candor to the tribunal (Rules 3.1, 3.3)** | You're responsible for everything you file; verify citations and factual assertions |
| **Supervision (Rules 5.1, 5.3)** | Establish policies and training for lawyers and staff using AI |
| **Fees (Rule 1.5)** | Bill only for time actually spent; don't charge for time AI saved; general overhead costs of AI tools usually aren't billable as expenses |

Many state bars (including California, Florida, New York and others) have issued their own guidance, and a growing number of courts require disclosure or certification of AI use in filings. Check local rules.

### England and Wales and elsewhere
The **Solicitors Regulation Authority (SRA)**, the **Law Society** and the **Bar Council** have published guidance emphasising the same themes: competence, confidentiality, supervision, accuracy and accountability. Notably, in May 2025 the SRA authorised **Garfield.Law**, described as the first law firm in England and Wales providing regulated services through an AI-driven model (focused on small debt recovery), with conditions: the system acts only on client approval, does not propose case law, and named solicitors remain accountable. Similar professional-conduct principles apply in Canada, Australia, India and the EU, with data protection laws (such as GDPR) adding requirements.

## The fake-citation problem (and how to avoid it)

The most notorious AI risk in law is **fabricated authority**. In *Mata v. Avianca* (S.D.N.Y. 2023), lawyers were sanctioned after filing a brief citing non-existent cases generated by ChatGPT. Since then, trackers such as Damien Charlotin's AI Hallucination Cases database have recorded **well over 1,500 cases worldwide** (as of mid-2026) where courts dealt with AI-generated errors, with sanctions, fee awards, referrals to regulators and public reprimands increasingly common.

**How to avoid it:**
1. **Never cite anything you haven't read** in an authoritative source.
2. **Use research tools grounded in legal databases** for case law, and still verify.
3. **Check quotations, pin cites and holdings,** not just that a case exists.
4. **Train everyone,** including support staff, on this risk.
5. **Add a citation-check step** to your filing workflow (some firms require a second-person check for AI-assisted filings).
6. **Comply with local disclosure rules** for AI-assisted filings.

## Confidentiality and governance: a firm AI policy

Before using AI with client material, a firm should decide and document:

1. **Approved tools,** and under what contractual terms (no training on firm data, retention limits, security certifications, data location).
2. **What data may be entered,** and what must be anonymised or never used.
3. **Who reviews AI output,** and how that review is recorded.
4. **How clients are informed** or consent is obtained where required (some clients' outside counsel guidelines restrict or require disclosure of AI use).
5. **Where data is processed,** for data-residency and cross-border transfer rules.
6. **Training requirements** for lawyers and staff.
7. **Incident handling** if confidential data is exposed.
8. **Billing rules** for AI-assisted work.

A written AI policy is now a basic part of firm risk management, and increasingly something clients ask to see.

Interestingly, Clio's 2025 research found that the share of legal professionals using **legal-specific** AI tools fell (to about 40%) even as overall AI use stayed high, suggesting many lawyers rely on general-purpose assistants. That makes firm policy and approved enterprise accounts even more important.

**Evaluation questions:** Does it train on our data? Where is data stored and for how long? What security certifications does the vendor hold? Can we export everything? How does it handle citations and sources? Can it integrate with our document and practice management systems? What does it cost per user, and what's the realistic adoption?

## Will lawyers be replaced by AI?

**Tasks will be replaced; the profession is unlikely to be.** Expect:

- **Shrinking routine work:** first-draft documents, basic research memos, document review, standard correspondence.
- **Changing junior roles:** less manual review, more supervision of AI output, earlier client exposure, and a real risk that juniors miss the "learning by doing" that builds expertise (see [How AI impacts skill formation](/blog/how-ai-impacts-skill-formation/)).
- **Pricing pressure:** clients will expect efficiency gains to show up in fees. Fixed fees and value pricing become more common than hourly billing for AI-accelerated work.
- **New services and players:** AI-enabled firms like Garfield.Law, legal products for consumers and small businesses, and in-house teams doing more themselves.
- **Higher value on human strengths:** judgement, strategy, negotiation, advocacy, empathy, ethics and accountability.

Lawyers who use AI well are likely to outcompete those who don't.

## How to get started (a 60-day plan for a small or mid-sized firm)

**Weeks 1–2:** write an AI policy; choose one approved enterprise AI assistant; run a training session on safe use and the citation risk.

**Weeks 3–4:** identify five high-volume tasks (e.g., client update emails, document summaries, first-draft letters, intake triage, time narratives) and create firm prompt templates for them.

**Weeks 5–8:** pilot with a small group; track time saved and quality; collect feedback; adjust the policy; expand to more users and one automated workflow (such as intake or document generation).

## Frequently asked questions

### Can lawyers use ChatGPT?
Yes, with care. Use business or enterprise versions with appropriate confidentiality terms rather than consumer accounts for client matters, follow your firm's AI policy and regulator guidance, and verify every citation and statement of law before relying on it.

### What is ABA Formal Opinion 512?
It is the American Bar Association's July 2024 ethics opinion on generative AI. It explains how duties of competence, confidentiality, client communication, candor to the tribunal, supervision and reasonable fees apply when lawyers use AI tools.

### Will lawyers be replaced by AI?
Routine drafting, research and document work will shrink, but judgement, advocacy, negotiation, client trust and accountability remain human. Lawyers who use AI effectively are likely to outcompete those who don't.

### Can ChatGPT give legal advice?
It can explain general legal concepts, but it isn't a substitute for advice from a qualified lawyer. It can be wrong, out of date, unaware of your jurisdiction, and it may invent authorities.

### Why have lawyers been sanctioned for using AI?
Because they filed documents containing fabricated cases, quotes or citations generated by AI without verifying them. Courts hold lawyers responsible for everything they file, regardless of how it was drafted.

### Do lawyers have to tell clients they use AI?
It depends on the jurisdiction, the circumstances and client agreements. ABA Opinion 512 says disclosure may be required in some situations, such as when AI use affects the representation or fees or when confidential information will be input; some clients' guidelines also require disclosure.

### Can lawyers bill for time saved by AI?
Under ABA Opinion 512, lawyers billing hourly may charge only for time actually spent, including time reviewing AI output, not for time that AI saved. Many firms are moving AI-accelerated work to fixed or value-based fees.
